# ASD-STE100 Writing Rules - Summary and Official Sources

This file summarizes the public description of ASD-STE100 (Simplified Technical English). It paraphrases rule categories and does not reproduce the standard's copyrighted text or ~900-word approved dictionary verbatim.

## Overview and Evolution

ASD-STE100 is a controlled natural language and international standard. **ASD (Aerospace, Security and Defence Industries Association of Europe, Brussels, Belgium)** owns the standard. The **ASD Simplified Technical English Maintenance Group (STEMG)** maintains it. European Union Trade Mark No. 017966390 protects Simplified Technical English and ASD-STE100.

- **Late 1970s - 1986:** AECMA (now ASD) developed STE with support from AIA. European airlines (AEA) requested it so non-native English technicians could read aircraft maintenance manuals safely. Released in 1986 as the *AECMA Simplified English Guide* (PSC-85-16598).
- **2005:** Elevated to an international specification (*ASD-STE100*).
- **2013 (Issue 6):** Made free of charge to download upon registration via STEMG (`https://www.asd-ste100.org/`).
- **January 15, 2025 (Issue 9):** Current official edition, designated as an **International Standard** (*ASD-STE100 Simplified Technical English*).

## Official Domains ("STE Today")

Per ASD STEMG (`https://www.asd-ste100.org/index.html`), four primary domains use STE:

1. **Aerospace and defense:** Core technical and maintenance documentation.
2. **Computer Science and Information Technology:** Optimization of human-machine interfaces (HMI), software documentation, and AI/FAIR data interoperability.
3. **Industry and services:** Manufacturing, engineering, and technical language services.
4. **Academia:** Research and technical communication education worldwide.

## Standard Structure (Issue 9)

- **53 writing rules across 9 sections** covering word choice, grammar, sentence structure, procedures, descriptive text, and safety warnings.
- **Approved dictionary (~900 words):** Each word has one approved meaning and one part of speech, with ~1,200 unapproved words and suggested replacements.
- **Project terminology allowance:** Organizations may maintain a controlled supplementary glossary of domain-specific technical nouns and verbs not covered by the base dictionary.

## Paraphrased Rule Categories

### Word Choice
- Use approved words only in their single approved meaning and part of speech.
- Prefer the plainest, shortest, most common word over formal synonyms.
- Use an approved verb to describe an action instead of a derived noun (Rule 3.7: write `Inspect the filter.`, not `Perform an inspection of the filter.`).
- Do not use phrasal verbs combining a verb and preposition (Rule 9.3: write `Remove the panel.`, not `Take off the panel.`).

### Verb Forms and Tenses
- Permitted forms: infinitive, imperative, simple present, simple past, simple future, and past participle used as an adjective.
- Exclude present perfect, past perfect, and progressive verb forms (`We received the report.`, not `We have received the report.`).
- Use `-ing` forms only as technical nouns or parts of technical noun phrases.

### Voice
- Use active voice for all procedures and instructions.
- Use passive voice only in descriptive prose when the actor is genuinely unknown or irrelevant.

### Sentence and Paragraph Structure
- One instruction per sentence.
- Maximum 20 words per sentence in procedures and instructions. Maximum 25 words per sentence in descriptive text.
- Never omit subjects, verbs, or articles (no ellipsis) to shorten sentences.
- Limit noun clusters to at most 3 consecutive nouns.
- Ban semicolons completely (Rule 8.1). Split clauses into separate sentences. All other standard punctuation marks remain permitted.
- Keep paragraphs to one topic and at most 6 sentences. Use vertical lists for 3 or more steps or conditions.

### Safety Instructions
- Start safety-critical instructions with a clear command or explicit condition rather than burying the hazard mid-sentence.
